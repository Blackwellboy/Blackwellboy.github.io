Available for

What I take on. Where a study on this page demonstrates it, it is linked.

Independent claim verification

Testing a vendor or community claim under controlled conditions, with the raw published whichever way it lands.

Shown by the HumanEval+ replication and the identity-prefix study, both of which came out against the claim under test.

Serving and deployment diagnosis

Working out why a model looks broken when the model is fine, which is usually a template, a parser or a history-handling problem rather than a weights problem.

Shown by multi-turn reasoning-stripping and the registry it fed.

Quantization verification

Taking a third-party quant and establishing whether it holds: that it builds and serves, what it costs in memory, and whether quality survives against the build you were already running.

Shown by the quant-floor study, which put a two-tier 3.25bpw expert quant against the NVFP4 build on the same harness. The kernel-path half of this is registry entry 10, the quant label is not the kernel path.

Long-duration production soaks

Running a model inside a real agent pipeline for as long as the question needs, and reporting drift, incidents and integrity behaviour rather than a throughput number.

Written up: the 12-hour soak.

Multi-node and tensor-parallel deployment

Bringing up and running models across networked nodes as tensor-parallel pairs: the fabric underneath, the topology, the serving config on top of it, and the failure modes that only appear once a model spans more than one box.

Shown by Hy3 on dual DGX Spark and MiniMax-M3 on dual DGX Spark.

Eval-harness and measurement auditing

Auditing a benchmark before you trust it: arm-level handicaps, scorer normalisation, contamination screens, and harnesses that quietly score a correct answer as wrong.

Shown by the evaluation category of the registry and by the HumanEval+ replication, where the original confound was a sampling difference between arms.

What a first pass looks like

Run minefield-doctor against your endpoint with --report and send me the output, along with the number that looks wrong and how you measured it. That output is a paste-ready block naming what passed, what failed and what could not be checked, so the first useful artifact exists before either of us has spent an hour on it.

Contact

Email or a direct message on X, whichever suits. Either way, say what stack you are on and which number looks wrong: that one line makes the first reply useful instead of a round of questions. If the finding is something the registry should carry, an issue on the registry is the public path, and contributions are credited by default.
